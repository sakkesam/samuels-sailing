To date we have done 2 offshore passages, our third is quickly approaching, that’s how late I am with writing this! First, I should specify what “offshore” is to us as I feel like it’s not universally defined (Is it?) and we appreciate that most people reading this, all 10 of you, mostly aren’t sailors. We classify it as offshore sailing if: we can’t see land, we are sailing overnight and we are off the grid in terms of phone signal. 

Our 2 passages should have been similar, they were similar locations, same time of year (ish) and similar distance. Spoiler alert: They were not. They could not have been more different if they tried. 

## The first passage:

Our first offshore passage was from Palamos, Spain, 130nm across the Gulf of Lyon to Marseille, France.
<Image
  src={routetomarseille}
  alt="Our track from Palamos to Marseille on noforeignland"
  class="blog-image"
/>
Our first “mistake” with this one was planning. We were on somewhat of a timeline as we had made plans to go to Switzerland to see Charlotte’s sister and thought the borderline spicy weather wouldn’t be too bad. We have since learnt that a boat on a deadline is a dangerous boat, and we now avoid making any solid plans. We’ve also learnt “it won’t be THAT bad” probably will be. 

Anyway, it was forecasted to be 15-20knots with a swell of about 1m. We saw there would big swell of about 1.7m outside of Hyeres, which is where we originally planned to go, so we changed our route to end up more north, in Marseille, to avoid the big swell.

### Calm beginnings and dolphins

It started out pretty uneventful when we set off at sunrise, the wind was so calm at the start that we actually had to engine for a few hours. We also got our first proper dolphin visit that was honestly breathtaking, and felt like a good omen.
<Image
  src={dolphin}
  alt="Dolphin swimming t the bow of our sailboat"
  class="blog-image"
/>
We had decent wind around late morning and early afternoon, then just as Spain was out of sight, things started to pick up.

### Into the storm

Full sails went to 1 reef then 2 pretty quickly. The sun set and the waves picked up, like really picked up. We were not blessed with any moonlight and in the pitch black we couldn’t see when the waves were coming nor from where. We found out when they crashed over the boat and sometimes into the cockpit. 

In addition to dealing with the growing wind, we were soaked. Just as we’d dry off, another wave would hit us in the face. We also soon noticed that the deck was covered in an extremely thick layer of salt? Yes , the sea is salty but we hadn’t had that happen before. Turns out, Gulf of Lyon has much higher salinity than most other areas in the Mediterranean so that added another fun element to this adventure.

### Battling the waves

For about 20 hours we were battling the waves while on a pretty steep heel, the autopilot was fighting for his life. We survived on Pringles and sweets. Charlotte tried to open a cupboard and discovered it full of broken glass, so we established we probably wouldn’t cook. Even opening the fridge was ridiculously tricky. Sleep was fully off the table, the boat was flying over the waves and the noise was deafening.

### Magic in the dark

However, as traumatic as all of this sounds, it has to be said that right in the middle of all the chaos something truly magical did happen. It was pitch dark (as previously mentioned) and the clouds cleared a large spot just above us, the most clear night sky appeared. With 0 land in sight and absolutely no light pollution for miles, the view was incredible. You could see the entire Milky Way. We thought we had seen pretty good stars in the Canary Islands and the Seychelles but honestly these were next level. 
<Image
  src={thumbsup}
  alt="Sakke in good spirits during a night sail"
  class="blog-image"
/>
It was when we were admiring the sky that Charlotte said “ why does the sea look blue?”. After being dismissed by logical Sakke, she continued to stare at the water and realised it was actually glowing. We put our extremely tired minds together and figured it was bioluminescence, which we later found out to be common in the Gulf of Lyon. The glowing blue waves hitting the boat were so magical, it suddenly didn’t seem too bad anymore. We sat in silence for probably 2 hours alternating staring at the sea and the sky, fully forgetting the weather that we were in.

### Arrival in Marseille

As we got closer to shore and were able to check the forecast, we found out that we were in that massive swell we purposely tried to avoid but land was in sight and things were looking up. After navigating through the busy Marseille shipping lane at sunrise, we arrived 32 hours after we had left Palamos. 
<Image
  src={tiredchar}
  alt="Charlotte being tired and ready for the sail to end"
  class="blog-image"
/>
We were totally exhausted, we didn’t leave the boat for about 3 days just trying to catch up on sleep and getting our lives together. It wasn’t the most ideal first offshore passage but it definitely isn’t one we will ever forget. 

## Second passage:

This part will be a lot shorter! Mostly because there’s honestly not a lot to report, as Charlottes dad constantly says, no news is good news. Our second passage was from Nice, France 100nm to Calvi, Corsica (also France). 

Having learnt from our previous mistake, we waited patiently for a good weather window for this crossing. However, we might have been a bit too cautious and decided to go when the forecast was a tad too light, with about 5-10knots of wind and virtually no swell. We also recruited reinforcements in forms of Sakkes dad.
<Image
  src={sakkedad}
  alt="Sakke and his dad enjoying the sail."
  class="blog-image"
/>
There was virtually no chance we were going to repeat passage number 1.

### Dolphins, turtles, and smooth sailing

We set off at sunrise again, wrapped in lots of layers, noticing that it was quite a bit colder than it had been recently and motor-sailed most of the day. Just before sunset we got another awesome visit from a huge pod of dolphins. They were everywhere, surrounding the boat for maybe 10-20 minutes which felt like hours. 

We also kept periodically seeing small turtles, at first we thought were seeing things because why would these small turtles just be miles out at sea right next to our boat? But after some googling we found out there are actually quite a few types of turtles in the med.

### A peaceful night watch

After a nutritious dinner of meal-prepped curry, we took shifts through the night to keep watch, not that there was a lot to watch for. With 3 people on board it also meant we all actually had some solid hours of rest. It helps that the boat wasn’t fighting for its life and we were very steady and quiet! 
<Image
  src={nightwatch}
  alt="Nightwatch together"
  class="blog-image"
/>
We were even blessed with a full moon that night so it was easy to keep an eye out for other boats and the non existent waves, everything went as smooth as possible. At about 4am we were able to turn the engine off for good and sail the remaining distance to Corsica.

### Sunrise in Calvi

We approached Calvi at sunrise, almost exactly 24 hours since we had left Nice. The sun was rising from behind the mountains over this picturesque town, there was a thin layer of fog over the beach which was lined with pine trees. It was an honestly unforgettable view to end our almost perfect passage. 
<Image
  src={swimming}
  alt="Charlotte swimming after our crossing from Nice to Corsica"
  class="blog-image"
/>
We dropped anchor in this stunning bay next to the worlds biggest sailing cruise ship (which made us feel very A. Poor and B. Small), hung out for a few hours then went into town for a late lunch of mussels and a couple of beers to celebrate. In an ideal world we could have had a few more knots of wind and saved a few litres of diesel but you can’t have everything. Big shoutout to Sakkes dad who came with us even though it meant he just had to take a flight right back home, things are definitely easier with another person, especially one that knows how to sail!
